# 2026-09-21 — one hundred and sixty years of H. G. Wells

He was born at Atlas House, 162 High Street, Bromley, on 21 September 1866, which
makes today his hundred-and-sixtieth birthday. Five scientific romances in the order
he wrote them, drawn the way his first illustrators drew them: line, and only line,
doing all the work. A different engraving trade in each slot — patent office, marine
survey, magazine illustration, sectional diagram, and a screened nocturne. The day
opens on the Time Machine in a Richmond laboratory and closes on the same machine at
the end of the world.

## 1. The Time Machine
A patent plate for a thing nobody could patent: the general arrangement, numbered,
keyed, and captioned in the deadpan register of the Victorian drawing office. The
quartz disc is built from twenty-eight shaded facets over a ring of contour lines,
and the dawn comes through the laboratory window as three flat yellow shafts with
motes turning over in them. Morning because this is the machine before it has gone
anywhere, and because the clock on the wall has already stopped.

## 2. Noble's Isle
Midday is when the survey party goes ashore and starts measuring, so Moreau's island
gets the full Admiralty treatment: coastline repeated in five thinning offsets to
make the water ring, cliff hachures, contour rings up to the peak, depth soundings,
a sixteen-point rose, rhumb lines, a scale of English miles, a graduated neat line,
and a section cut on the line A–B. Prendick's track inland is the red dotted line.
The green is the only flat colour on the sheet, which is what makes the island read
as an island from across the room.

## 3. The War of the Worlds
The one piece that isn't a diagram — a full-bleed illustration in the manner of the
inky 1906 Alvim Corrêa edition, which Wells thought did more for the book than his
own prose had. Red is doing three jobs at once: the sky, the joints turning inside
the black legs, and the weed on the near bank. The tripods are generated from a few
parameters each, so they stand differently. Afternoon because this is the hour the
valley empties.

## 4. The First Men in the Moon
A vertical section, evening being the hour when everything above ground goes dim and
everything below it is lit. The rock is the flat colour, the galleries are cut out of
it in white, and yellow appears only at the lamps and in the Hall of the Grand Lunar.
Bedford's sphere sits on the crater floor with the lid thrown back on its hinge, and
the Earth hangs where our moon should be. The Selenites are drawn at the scale a
section drawing allows them, which is very small indeed.

## 5. The Further Vision
Thirty million years downstream, on the beach at the end of the world. Made without
SVG: the light is a continuous field computed in numpy — the dull red dome on the
horizon, its halo dying off in the cold air, the broken column on the flat sea — and
then screened through an 8×8 Bayer matrix before it ever meets the palette, so the
gradient arrives as a print texture rather than a dither laid over a photograph. Only
the silhouettes are ink. Eighty-eight per cent black, which is what night should be.

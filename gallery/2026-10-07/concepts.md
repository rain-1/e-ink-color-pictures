# 2026-10-07 — The Far Side

Seed: on this date in 1959 Luna 3 photographed the far side of the Moon for the first time.
It used a 35 mm camera, developed the film on board, scanned it with a cathode-ray tube, and
sent it home like a fax. New moon is on the 10th, so this week the side we never see is
almost fully lit. The owner asked for much more detail and for restricted palettes only
occasionally, so every piece today uses most of the six inks and is drawn densely.

## 1. The Pad
A full-colour illustration in the style of a 1950s gouache poster: the R-7 on its launch
mount (the "tulip") at Tyuratam at first light. It has lattice gantries, venting oxygen, a
railway running into the flame pit, feather grass, trucks, tiny engineers, and a waning
crescent in the east (accurate for this morning). It's the morning slot because it's the
beginning: the sun is rising and the rocket hasn't left yet.

## 2. The Station
An engineering plate: Luna 3 drawn half as polished skin and half as a cutaway, with 14
numbered callouts covering the camera, film magazine, developer bath, flying-spot scanner
and radio. Insets show the figure-eight trajectory and a strip of 35 mm film. Midday is for
reading; this is the piece to stand close to.

## 3. The Map
A geologic map of the far side in the manner of the USGS lunar sheets. The units are
patterned fills in all six inks over about 2,600 modelled craters, with real basins in
their real places (Moscoviense, Tsiolkovskiy, Korolev, Apollo, South Pole–Aitken), plus a
graticule and an explanation box. It's the bright afternoon reading of the day's subject:
what we know now.

## 4. Frame 26
The same modelled Moon rendered as Luna 3 sent it: grainy, scan-lined, with a dropout,
Floyd–Steinberg dithered in black, blue and white inside a film rebate. Over it is the 1959
atlas key in yellow and red, including the red dashed boundary of the near side and VI,
the "Soviet Mountains". That range turned out to be a bright ray, not mountains. Evening is
when the room dims, and this is the day's first dark piece.

## 5. Simeiz
Night at the Crimean observatory where the signals were received: domes on Koshka mountain
with the slit of one open and lit, a receiving antenna, cypresses, a house with lit windows,
and someone on the terrace looking up. Overhead is a moonless sky with the Milky Way and its
dark rift, Draco, and Draconid meteors (they peak on the 9th). Mostly black, with a little
light in it.
